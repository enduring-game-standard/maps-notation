# How music theory keeps analytic layers grounded and comparable

Research for [issue #19](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/19), feeding [#14](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/14) (map: [#3](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/3)). The question: how do Schenkerian analysis, Roman numeral analysis, and Forte's pitch-class set theory each keep an analytic layer tied to the score and comparable across pieces? For each one, how does a layer point back to the score, what makes two analyses comparable, and how is it drawn?

**Source caveat, up front.** I could not read the three named primary books directly. *Der freie Satz* (1935) is on IMSLP, but the site's bot wall blocked the download. The Internet Archive copies of Forte's *The Structure of Atonal Music* (1973) and Forte & Gilbert's *Introduction to Schenkerian Analysis* (1982) are lend-only, and full-text search was refused (HTTP 403). The Google Books API was over quota. Grove was not reached. So the Schenker and Forte claims below rest on (a) open scholarly sources that quote or encode those books (Kirlin's dissertation, Pankhurst's SchenkerGUIDE, Open Music Theory), (b) a primary specification for Roman numerals (RomanText, ISMIR 2019), and (c) my own computation of the set-class tables. Each claim is tagged with where it comes from. Section 5 lists what stays unverified.

## Summary: what bears on MAPS

1. **The score is the only authority, and every layer cites it by address.** All three systems treat the surface as ground truth. Analytic layers are claims about it. Roman numerals cite *measure + beat* (RomanText: `m5 b1 IV6 b2 V`). A Schenkerian reduction cites *specific notes* (Kirlin's corpus: `X (Y) Z`, meaning "the notes Y prolong the motion from note X to note Z"). Set analysis cites *a segment*, a named group of notes in the score. MAPS should give every element a stable address, and every higher-layer element should list the addresses of the lower-layer elements it stands for.
2. **Judgment and mechanism sit in separate steps.** In set theory, the only judgment is *segmentation* (which notes go together). After that, normal order, prime form, and the Forte name are all computed. Roman numerals put judgment in three named places (harmonic vs. non-harmonic notes, which chord an incomplete sonority implies, which key) and then use a fixed grammar. Schenker left the judgment undefined, and that is the method's best-known weakness. MAPS should make the authored choice explicit and small (what to fold together) and compute everything after it.
3. **A reduction is a strict nesting, and every element must be accounted for.** Pankhurst's presentation rules: every note "must be shown to prolong or decorate the background either directly or by being an elaboration of an elaboration". No note may be left "hanging in mid air". Elaborations "may share a first and last note", but "no two elaborations can overlap". Yust's formalization (via Kirlin) makes this exact: a reduction is a maximal outerplanar graph (MOP), and each triangle is one elaboration, with two parent notes and one child. For MAPS, folds should form a laminar family (nested or disjoint, never crossing), and the checker can enforce that each lower element is either kept or explained.
4. **Comparability comes from ending every analysis in a tiny fixed vocabulary.** Every Schenkerian analysis ends in one of three backgrounds (the 3-, 5-, or 8-line *Ursatz*). Every Roman numeral analysis uses one closed grammar relative to a key, so a progression in C and one in F♯ read the same. Every set analysis lands in one of 224 set classes, with a catalog name such as `3-11`. Two pieces are compared by the labels they reach, not by how their scores look.
5. **Name a class by a catalog index, and keep the tie-break rule fixed.** Forte and Rahn break prime-form ties differently, and they disagree on the prime-form spelling of exactly 6 of the 208 classes of size 3 to 9. I computed this (section 3.4). Both agree on the Forte *names*. I also found one open textbook (Puget Sound) that says it uses Rahn's method but prints Forte's spelling for all six. The catalog name is the stable key. The canonical string depends on a convention. MAPS identity needs one frozen canonicalization rule plus a short registry name.
6. **Invariants filter, but they don't identify.** Forte's interval-class vector is a cheap invariant, but 23 pairs of distinct classes share one (the "Z-relation", computed below). This is the same lesson as NKS's node and edge counts ([#6](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/6)): print invariants beside a design, but decide identity by canonical form.
7. **Drawing: one shared time axis, structural weight shown by glyph, not color.** Schenkerian levels are stacked and "aligned properly so they line up with each other (and the music if included)", with measure numbers marked. Note value shows structural rank (open notehead with stem and beam for the background, filled notehead for lower levels). Slurs and beams draw the elaboration relation. Roman numerals go under the staff at the chord's onset, with the key at the start. For MAPS, a kept element should sit at the same position in every layer, so a reader's eye can drop straight down through the layers.
8. **Disagreement is data, stored side by side.** RomanText has `m1var1` for alternative readings, `m3-4 = m1-2` for exact repeats, and an `Analyst:` field. When in Rome stores second analyses of the same work as `analysis_B.txt`. Kirlin compares two Schenkerian analyses by counting shared triangles and shared internal edges. MAPS can give two foldings of the same net the same treatment: keep both, and diff them by shared groupings.

## Detail

### 1. Schenkerian analysis: successive reductions

**Levels.** Schenker's *Der freie Satz* (Vienna: Universal Edition, 1935; 2nd ed. by Oswald Jonas, 1956; trans. Ernst Oster as *Free Composition*, Longman, 1979) "is divided into three parts, dealing respectively with background, middleground, and foreground levels of structure" ([Wikipedia, "Free Composition"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Composition), secondhand). Pankhurst defines the terms: "The surface of the music is called the *foreground*, the deepest layer the *background* and those layers of elaborations in between are referred to as the *middleground*" ([SchenkerGUIDE, "What is Schenkerian analysis?"](https://www.schenkerguide.com/whatisschenkeriananalysis.php)).

**How each level relates to the one below.** Kirlin: "the analysis procedure begins from a musical score and proceeds in a reductive manner, eliminating notes at each successive level of the hierarchy until a fundamental background structure is reached. At a given level, the notes that are preserved to the next highest-level are said to be more structural than the notes not retained." From the top down, "each subsequent level of the hierarchy expands, or prolongs, the content of the previous level". Kirlin quotes that line from Forte, "Schenker's Conception of Musical Structure", *Journal of Music Theory* 3 (1959). Source: P. B. Kirlin, *A Probabilistic Model of Hierarchical Music Analysis*, PhD diss., UMass Amherst, 2014, pp. 4-5 ([PDF](https://www.cs.rhodes.edu/~kirlinp/kirlin14probabilistic-condensed.pdf)). Elaborations come in a small set of named types: neighbor notes, passing-note progressions, and arpeggiations ([SchenkerGUIDE](https://www.schenkerguide.com/whatisschenkeriananalysis.php)).

**The fixed background.** Schenker's analyses "always reduced a composition to one of three specific musical patterns at the most abstract level". Each is a descending *Urlinie* (fundamental line) over a *Bassbrechung* (bass arpeggiation, I-V-I), and together they form the *Ursatz*. Kirlin's Figure 1.1 shows the three: 3̂-2̂-1̂, 5̂-4̂-3̂-2̂-1̂, and 8̂-7̂-…-1̂ (Kirlin pp. 4-5). Kirlin notes that "this idea has proved much more controversial than that of structural levels." Wikipedia attributes the rule that "the fundamental line begins with scale degree 8, 5 or 3, and moves to 1" to *Free Composition* §10, and Schenker's motto "Semper idem, sed non eodem modo" (always the same, but not in the same way) to §29, p. 18. Both are secondhand ([Wikipedia, "Schenkerian analysis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schenkerian_analysis)).

**How a layer points back to the score.**
- *By note identity, with full coverage required.* Pankhurst's rules: "make sure that any note of a graph can be related, however tortuously, back to the Ursatz - every note must be shown to prolong or decorate the background either directly or by being an elaboration of an elaboration"; "ensure that notes in the middle and background are clearly present in and aligned with the foreground analysis"; don't "leave notes hanging in mid air with no slur, beam or stem to explain them" ([SchenkerGUIDE, "Presentational DOs and DON'Ts"](https://www.schenkerguide.com/schenkerdosanddonts.php)).
- *By strict nesting.* "While two may share a first and last note and a note in the middle of one elaboration may be decorated by another, no two elaborations can overlap so that they suggest a contradictory function for shared notes" (same page).
- *As a data structure.* Yust (2006, *Formal Models of Prolongation*, PhD diss., Univ. of Washington; read here only through Kirlin) represents a reduction as a maximal outerplanar graph. The vertices are the notes in time order, plus a `Start` and a `Finish` pseudo-event. The perimeter edges join consecutive notes. "Each triangle in the polygon specifies a prolongation… the child note y has two parent notes, x and z." "Edges closer to the root edge express more abstract relationships." Outerplanarity is "necessary for us to enforce the strict hierarchy among the prolongations" (Kirlin pp. 16-18). Yust treats analyses with untriangulated "holes" as incomplete. A one-parent elaboration, such as an incomplete neighbor, is handled by eliding the missing parent with a nearby structural note (Kirlin p. 17).
- *As a text format.* Kirlin's corpus encodes analyses as `X (Y) Z`, "where X and Z are individual notes in the score and Y is a non-empty list of notes… the notes in Y prolong the motion from note X to note Z". One of X or Z may be omitted (an incomplete prolongation). The scores themselves are stored as MusicXML. The corpus holds 41 excerpts, 907 notes, and 792 prolongations, with analyses drawn from Forte & Gilbert, Cadwallader & Gagné, SchenkerGUIDE, and one expert (Kirlin pp. 26-28).

**What makes two analyses comparable.**
- *Across pieces*, the backgrounds come from a closed set of three, and middleground elaborations come from a small set of named types. Kirlin's corpus statistics go further. A prolongation is reduced to an interval triple (parent, child, child) that ignores direction, octave, and quality. For example, (C, D, E), (E, D, C), and (C, D♭, E♭) are all one type, "identical under octave displacement" (Kirlin p. 28). That is a canonical form for elaborations.
- *Across analysts*, Kirlin compares two analyses of the same excerpt by *triangle accuracy* (the share of triangles they have in common) and *edge accuracy* (the share of internal edges they have in common). Edge accuracy exists because two MOPs can share a deep connection while sharing no triangle at all (Kirlin p. 49).
- *The limit.* "Schenker declined to give any methodical description of his procedure, and therefore everyone does Schenkerian analysis slightly differently… there can be multiple possible 'correct' analyses for a single composition" (Kirlin p. 7). Kirlin also quotes Forte (1959): "the important deficiencies… arise from his failure to define with sufficient rigor the conditions under which particular structural events occur" (Kirlin p. 5). Lerdahl and Jackendoff's *Generative Theory of Tonal Music* (1983) formalized reductions as preference rules, but conceded that their "theory cannot provide a computable procedure for determining musical analyses" (as quoted by Kirlin p. 11).

**How it is drawn.** From SchenkerGUIDE's [notation summary](https://www.schenkerguide.com/schenkernotationsummary.php) and [DOs and DON'Ts](https://www.schenkerguide.com/schenkerdosanddonts.php):
- *Background.* The Urlinie gets "open noteheads, stems and a beam, with each note of the descent marked with a caretted number" (scale degree). The Bassbrechung gets "stems and a beam". An interruption is marked "||".
- *Middleground.* "a combination of slurs and stems"; "solid slurs group together adjacent notes, dotted slurs… connections between notes that are separated"; arrows for register shifts; and labels ("N, 3-prg etc.") for the elaboration type.
- *Alignment.* "align the different levels properly so they line up with each other (and the music if included)"; "clearly mark bar/measure numbers"; and don't "include bar lines or rests".
- *Rhythmic values show rank, not rhythm.* Open noteheads usually mark "the highest structural level, and filled-in noteheads for tones of lower levels" (Cadwallader & Gagné, *Analysis of Tonal Music*, appendix, as quoted by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schenkerian_analysis); not checked against the book). Schenker himself wrote in 1932 that graphic presentation "has now been developed to a point that makes an explanatory text unnecessary" (*Fünf Urlinie-Tafeln*, foreword, via Wikipedia).

### 2. Roman numeral analysis: one layer, a fixed grammar, a score address

**What a label is.** A Roman numeral gives a chord's triad type (by letter case and suffix), its root's scale degree relative to the tonic, its inversion, and any sevenths or alterations (Tymoczko, Gotham, Cuthbert & Ariza, "The RomanText Format", *Proc. ISMIR* 2019, pp. 123-129, CC BY 4.0, [PDF](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2019/paper/000012.pdf)). "The key is labeled at the start of the analysis. Roman numerals are written underneath the staff" ([Open Music Theory, "Roman Numerals"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/roman-numerals/)).

**Where judgment sits.** RomanText names three layers of judgment: "which notes are harmonic / non-harmonic; what complete harmony, if any, an incomplete or otherwise ambiguous chord might represent; and the underlying keys and when they change." So "humans may legitimately disagree about the best analysis of a given passage" (p. 124). Corpora state their policy up front. Theirs prefers harmony changes on strong beats, analyzing "similar material in similar ways", and readings consistent with standard theory (p. 128).

**Derivation in both directions (the important asymmetry).** "Given a Roman numeral and a key, one can algorithmically derive an absolute chord label… one can translate from Roman numerals to absolute labels but not vice versa." Function labels (T, S, D) are one step more abstract: "many distinct harmonies map to a single functional term, [so] these labels cannot be translated into Roman numerals or absolute chord labels. However, function labels can often be recovered from Roman numerals" (p. 123). The result is a chain of three lossy, downward-computable layers: chord, then Roman numeral, then function. Each layer can be computed from the one below it once the judgment (the key) is fixed.

**How it points back to the score.** Every line is addressed by measure and beat. "Each line of analysis starts with the symbol 'm' followed immediately by a measure number… pairs of beat numbers (preceded by 'b') and their corresponding Roman numerals": `m5 b1 IV6 b2 V` (p. 125). The standard pins down the details:
- Measure 1 is "always the first full measure", and a pickup is `m0`.
- Repeat endings get letters (`216a`, `216b`).
- Beats follow the meter, so 6/8 runs `1, 1.33, 1.66, 2…`.
- A key change sits between beat and chord (`m112 IV6 b4 C: ii`), and a pivot chord carries both readings (`m112 IV6 b4 vi C: ii`).
- An uncertain secondary key is written `?(Bb:`.
- Phrase boundaries are `||`, pedals are spans (`Pedal: G m14 b3 m19 b1`), and form labels go on their own line (`Form: Development`).
- Repeats use `m3-4 = m1-2`, and alternative readings use `m1var1 ii` (pp. 125-127).

When the encoded score exists, music21 renders the analysis "as an additional 'part' in order to view the relationship between score and analysis in musical notation" (p. 127).

**What makes analyses comparable.** Key-relative labels are transposition-invariant: "Roman numerals are based on scale degrees rather than specific pitch names, [so] they are useful for understanding how harmonies function similarly in different keys" ([Open Music Theory](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/roman-numerals/)). Machine comparison needs a shared syntax. The RomanText authors say debates about analysis "are exacerbated by the absence of a public corpus of expert Roman numeral analyses, and by the more fundamental lack of an agreed-upon, computer-readable syntax" (abstract). The successor meta-corpus [When in Rome](https://github.com/MarkGotham/When-in-Rome) holds about 2,000 analyses of 1,500 works in one format, one folder layout (`<genre>/<composer>/<set>/<movement>/`), and one file per analysis. A second analyst's reading is stored beside the first as `analysis_B.txt` (the TAVERN dataset has such pairs). An automatic analysis is stored in the same format, so it can be diffed against the human one. The README suggests using an existing analysis "as a kind of template for your own, by creating a copy and editing only the moments you disagree with."

### 3. Forte's pitch-class set theory: algorithmic canonical names after one human choice

**The one human choice is segmentation.** "The process of dividing a passage or piece of music into its component parts." OMT warns that because set relations are factual, "it's easy to end up with an unfalsifiable analysis that may still be ineffective", and it lists the grounds for grouping: contiguity, rhythm, meter, timbre, articulation, and register. It also says: "Your relationships should all involve notes that actually appear in the score". It quotes Straus: "You can't pluck notes out in some random way, just because they form a set that you are interested in" ([OMT, "Analyzing with Set Theory (or not!)"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/analyzing-with-set-theory/)).

**Everything after segmentation is computed.**
- *Normal order* is "the most compressed way to write a given collection of pitch classes, in ascending order". OMT compares it to root position: "a standard way to order the pitch classes… so that we can classify and compare them easily" ([OMT, "Pc Sets, Normal Order, and Transformations"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/pc-sets-normal-order-and-transformations/)).
- *A set class* is "a group of pitch-class sets related by transposition or inversion". It is named by its *prime form*, "the version of the set that is transposed to zero and is most compact to the left (compared with its inversion)". OMT's procedure has five steps (normal order, transpose to 0, invert and take normal order, transpose to 0, pick the more left-packed). Its worked example turns [10, 0, 2, 3, 5] into (02357). OMT adds that the prime form "is just a label for a set class. It does not have any special status" ([OMT, "Set Class and Prime Form"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/set-class-and-prime-form/)).
- *The Forte name.* "When Allen Forte created a catalog of every possible 3-, 4-, 5-, 6-, 7-, 8-, and 9-note set in Appendix 1 of *The Structure of Atonal Music*, he labeled each prime form with two numbers separated by a hyphen… now known as 'Forte numbers'" ([Hutchinson, *Music Theory for the 21st-Century Classroom*, §33.5](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/ForteNumbers.html)). Tables pair each class with its complement and print its interval-class vector, as in `(037) 3-11 <001110>`.

**3.4 Computed check of the canonical forms.** I wrote a short script (all 4,096 subsets of 12 pitch classes, both tie-break rules) and confirmed the following:
- 352 classes under transposition alone and 224 under transposition and inversion, counting the empty set. That is 12, 29, 38, 50, 38, 29, and 12 classes for sizes 3 to 9, so 208 in Forte's range.
- **Forte's and Rahn's tie-breaks give different prime-form strings for exactly 6 classes:** 5-20 (Forte 01378 / Rahn 01568), 6-Z29 (013689 / 023679), 6-31 (013589 / 014579), 7-Z18 (0123589 / 0145679), 7-20 (0124789 / 0125679), and 8-26 (0124579T / 0134578T). OMT's table prints the Rahn strings with the same Forte numbers. [Hutchinson's list](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/ListsOfSetClasses.html) says "These lists use prime forms as calculated using the Rahn method", but it prints the Forte strings for all six (checked against the fetched page). So the prime-form string depends on a convention, even inside textbooks, while the catalog name is stable.
- **The Z-relation.** 46 classes of size 3 to 9 (23 pairs) share an interval-class vector with a class they are not equivalent to. Examples are 4-Z15 and 4-Z29, and 6-Z3 and 6-Z36. The vector is an invariant, not an identity.

**How it is drawn.** In the analytic practice I could check (OMT's worked Bartók and Debussy examples), segments are marked on the score and labeled with their normal order or set class. Relations between segments are drawn as labeled arrows (T₅, I₈, T₄). The label sits on the music, and the catalog does the comparing. I could not check Forte's own figure conventions in the 1973 book.

**Comparability across pieces.** Two passages in different pieces that both yield 3-11 (major or minor triad) or 6-Z44 are the "same" object at this level, whatever their spelling, register, or order. Forte went on to group classes into *set complexes* (K and Kh, through inclusion with complements) to relate a whole piece's sets to each other. I know this from general knowledge and did not verify it against the book (see section 5).

### 4. Read against MAPS's own framing

`GAME_ABSTRACTION_LEVELS.md` uses these analogies, and the sources support most of them, with two refinements:
- It says Roman numerals are "not 'derived from' the score by an algorithm" and that analysts disagree. RomanText supports that, but it also shows *where* the judgment goes (non-harmonic notes, implied chords, keys) and that the label can be computed *downward* once those choices are made. This is a better model for a MAPS Level 4 than "independently authored". The analysis is a small set of explicit authored choices plus computed consequences.
- It cites Forte as "grounded in the score but independent of it." More precisely, Forte's vocabulary is entirely mechanical once a segment is chosen. The only analyst freedom is segmentation. That is the clean precedent for MAPS: authored grouping, computed naming.

## Transferable mechanisms for MAPS

1. **A reduction cites the layer below by name, in the form of `X (Y) Z`.** A folded verb or state in a higher layer lists the lower-layer verbs and states it stands for. The text form could be `fold attack (wind_up swing recover)`. The checker verifies three things: every cited name exists one layer down, every lower element is either *kept* (it shows up again in the higher layer) or *explained* by exactly one fold, and nothing is left hanging (Pankhurst's rule).
2. **Folds nest strictly.** Two folds may share boundary states (elaborations "may share a first and last note"), but they may not cross. Formally, the folded sets form a laminar family, the analog of MOP outerplanarity. This keeps unfolding unique and lets reachability, stall, and dead-verb checks run per layer.
3. **Keep the judgment small and explicit, and compute the rest.** Treat grouping (segmentation) as the only authored act in a layer. Everything else about the higher layer is derived from the grouping and the net below: its arcs, its counts, and its class name. That matches RomanText's split (key is a choice, chord content is derived) and Forte's (segment is a choice, name is derived).
4. **Give canonical forms frozen equivalences, a frozen tie-break, and a registry name.** State which operations count as "the same design": renaming states and verbs, permuting seats, and perhaps mirror symmetry (MAPS's Tn and I). Fix one canonical-labeling rule and never change it. Publish a catalog index (a short name, Forte's `3-11` or Wolfram's short codes) as the identity readers quote. Keep the canonical string as a derived artifact, since 6 of 208 Forte classes show that two reasonable tie-breaks give different strings.
5. **Print invariants beside each design, but don't use them as identity.** Counts such as states, verbs, arcs by kind, positions, and endings play the role of Forte's interval vector. They are a fast filter. The Z-relation shows that equal invariants don't prove equal structure, so identity still needs the canonical form.
6. **End every top layer in a small, fixed vocabulary.** The Ursatz has three forms, and Roman numerals are a closed grammar relative to a key. Comparability comes from every analysis landing in the same small alphabet. MAPS's top layer for a moveset should likewise use a fixed, enumerated set of shapes (for example, the idle-loop kinds from [#8](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/8)). Name elaboration types the way Schenkerian graphs label "N" and "3-prg", and reduce them to a canonical type for corpus counts, as Kirlin's interval triples do.
7. **Draw layers stacked on one shared axis.** A kept element sits at the same horizontal position in every layer, so it lines up vertically with itself below. Show structural rank with a fixed glyph scale (open, stemmed, filled), not with color. Draw the "explains" relation as a bracket or slur from the folded element to the span it covers. Mark element addresses the way graphs mark measure numbers. The axis needs a design decision: music has time, while a MAPS net needs something like distance from idle.
8. **Store alternative readings and second analysts side by side, and diff them.** Adopt `var` for alternative foldings and `= ` for "this region is an exact repeat of that one" (RomanText's `m3-4 = m1-2`, which is MAPS's collapse of repeats, as in Boss Keys). Record `analyst:` metadata. Compare two foldings of one net by shared groupings and shared internal connections, like Kirlin's triangle and edge accuracy, so that disagreement can be measured.
9. **Keep the downward derivation computable.** As with chord, Roman numeral, and function, each MAPS layer should be computable from the layer below once the authored grouping is fixed. Unfolding (going up the chain toward the full net) restores detail, because the citations name it.
