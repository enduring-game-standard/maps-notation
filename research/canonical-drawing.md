# Canonical drawing conventions outside music

Research for [issue #20](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/20). It feeds [#14](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/14), and the map is [#3](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/3). The question: in chemical structure diagrams, electrical schematics, Hasse diagrams, and Feynman diagrams, which conventions make two diagrams comparable by shape? For each one, what fixes position, what is left free, and what can a reader learn from shape alone?

This note builds on two sibling findings and tries not to repeat them. [Boss Keys](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/blob/research/boss-keys-graphs/research/boss-keys-graphs.md) found one meaningful axis, a closed symbol set, and collapsed repeats. [NKS](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/blob/research/nks-side-by-side/research/nks-side-by-side.md) found a fixed comparison protocol and node positions that carry no meaning.

## Summary: what bears on MAPS layout

1. **No notation puts identity in the coordinates.** A molecule is its bond graph, and IUPAC says rotating a structure "will never affect its chemical meaning", with Fischer projections as the one exception. A Hasse diagram is its cover relation. A Feynman diagram is its topology, and the rule is to draw "all of the topologically distinct" diagrams. A schematic is its netlist. Position is presentation in all four, which matches the NKS finding.
2. **Each notation ties at most one or two axes to a meaning and leaves the rest free.** In a Hasse diagram, up means greater, and this is part of the definition. Everything else is free. In schematics, signal runs left to right and positive supply sits at the top, but this is advice ("don't be dogmatic"). Feynman's original diagrams had time on the vertical axis. Today's diagrams are drawn "in a more stylized way, highlighting the topology". Chemistry has no axis meaning. It has only orientation preferences: main axis horizontal, principal groups toward the right, rings toward the bottom left.
3. **The free axis is filled by idioms, not by an algorithm.** Comparability comes from canonical shapes for recurring substructures. Benzene "points up and down". A six-membered ring has a vertical bond at its left. A fused ring system has the most rings in a horizontal row. A differential amplifier drawn the wrong way "won't be easily recognized". A flip-flop has clock and inputs on the left and outputs on the right. Lattice theorists know the 8-element Boolean lattice is drawn as a cube. A channel diagram "looks like the letter T". Brown's graphs are the same thing in the game domain.
4. **How firm a convention is decides how well it survives.** Where the axis is definitional (Hasse) or carries meaning (Fischer, Haworth), it holds. Where it is advisory, practice drifts. Kaiser shows Feynman diagrams splitting into local school styles within a decade, so that Cornell's looked unlike Columbia's or Chicago's. IUPAC calls orientation "an art form" and its own rules "only guidelines".
5. **A derived layout that is correct can still be useless. This is the user's "ordered mesh" warning, and order theory states it outright.** Freese writes: "any permutation of the elements of a particular level would produce a correct representation of the order, [but] almost any such permutation would make the diagram unrecognizable", and "a randomly chosen diagram will be mathematically correct, [but] it is very likely to be useless." Even with heights fixed, minimizing crossings is NP-complete. His fix has two parts. A deterministic rank function fixes the meaningful axis. Forces arrange the free axis, and the result is "recognizably the same" as hand drawings but not as good. TikZ-Feynman's spring layout fails exactly where convention wants a direction, as in a decay drawn with the parent on the left and the daughters on the right. Its fallbacks are a layered layout, then manual placement.
6. **Cycles get a fixed shape, not an axis.** Hasse diagrams and Brown's graphs need acyclic, monotone structure. Chemistry, whose structures are full of cycles, draws a ring as a regular polygon in a set orientation. A ring has "fewer degrees of freedom" than a chain, and readers recognize it. *(My inference, not a claim from these sources.)* A MAPS net has cycles everywhere, such as the turn token passing back and forth. A cycle needs its own fixed shape, because it cannot be forced onto an order axis.
7. **To compare, align a related set on a shared core.** IUPAC says a series of related molecules should share one chosen "principal" ring system, even one that would lose in isolation, and that "a group of related structures should be consistent in the style that is used." When a diagram shows relationships, as in a reaction, each part may leave its preferred orientation to show the relationship. Comparability is a property of a set drawn together, not of each drawing alone.
8. **One mark, one meaning.** IUPAC: "the end of one bond should not touch the end of another unless they truly are both bound to the same atom". Dashed bonds are not acceptable for coordination because dashes already mean stereochemistry. Horowitz and Hill: "Always use the same symbol for the same device", and four wires must not meet at one point. Feynman practice fixes a line style per particle kind. Shape alone cannot tell a t-channel diagram from a u-channel one, which differ only in which outgoing labels attach where. So the roles must be visible in the marks, not left to placement. *(My inference for MAPS.)* The difference between a seat verb and a world verb should be in the glyph, not only in its position or color.

**What this suggests for #14. This is inference, not a decision:**

- Give MAPS one definitional axis, meaning something structure fixes, computed deterministically. Rank by conversational order is one candidate: seat utterance, then world response. Rank by first-reachable phase, Brown's analogue, is another.
- Fill the free axis with a small library of named idioms: the turn ring, the board family, the judge column. Add side conventions on the model of "principal groups toward the right". One example would be seat verbs on one side and world verbs on the other.
- Draw cycles as fixed shapes.
- Keep the authored `at x y` as a stored override, the way TikZ-Feynman falls back to manual placement, but do not treat it as identity.
- In comparison mode, align designs on a shared core.

Without the idioms and the axis meaning, a derived layout is Freese's "correct but useless" diagram.

## Detail

### 1. Chemical structure diagrams (IUPAC Recommendations 2008)

Source: J. Brecher, "Graphical representation standards for chemical structure diagrams (IUPAC Recommendations 2008)", *Pure Appl. Chem.* 80(2), 277–410, doi:[10.1351/pac200880020277](https://doi.org/10.1351/pac200880020277). I read the full text from a copy hosted at EPFL (<https://www.epfl.ch/labs/lcso/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/IUPAC-drawing-rules.pdf>). Section numbers are IUPAC's.

**What the standard is for.** The abstract gives three principal recommendations: "Know your audience", "Avoid ambiguous drawing styles", and "Avoid inconsistent drawing styles." The introduction says: "Production of good chemical structure depictions will likely always remain something of an art form. There are few cases where it can be said that a specific representation is 'right' and that all others are 'wrong'. These guidelines do not try to do that." The goal is drawings "likely to be interpreted the same way by most chemists" that also look "good-looking" (GR-0). Examples are graded "preferred", "acceptable", "not acceptable", or "wrong". "Wrong" is reserved for drawings that are self-contradictory or "accurately represent a molecule other than the one intended" (GR-0.1). One "preferred" diagram "does not preclude the possibility of other 'preferred' diagrams" (GR-0.1). So IUPAC does not define one canonical picture.

**Position carries no identity.** GR-3 says: "as far as chemical meaning is concerned, [orientations] do not matter. With a single exception in the case of Fischer projections, simple rotation of a chemical structure within the plane of the diagram will never affect its chemical meaning ... In terms of chemical meaning, the orientation of structures is irrelevant." Orientation still matters for reading: "most chemists would expect to see [benzene] with 'points up and down'". A reasonable orientation exists "to present chemical information in a way that is most convenient and least surprising to other chemists" (GR-3).

**What is fixed by convention (soft):**
- Global orientation: "structures should be oriented so that their main axis is horizontal" (GR-3.1.1). "Principal groups should be toward the right" (GR-3.1.2), so systematic numbering runs right to left. "Rings should be toward the bottom-left" (GR-3.1.3). A branching double bond goes upward (GR-3.2.2).
- Local geometry: one bond length for most bonds (GR-1.1). Two-bond atoms at 120°, with "a trans-like depiction ... strongly preferred and arbitrary 'bends' in the chain ... avoided" (GR-4.1.1). Bonds are drawn collinear "if and only if they connect collinearly in three dimensions" (GR-4.1).
- Ring templates: isolated rings of 3, 4, 5, 7, or 8 atoms have a horizontal bond at the top or bottom. Six-membered rings have "a vertical bond at the left of the ring" (GR-3.4.2). Rings have "fewer degrees of freedom" than chains, and "it is especially important to depict rings correctly, since readers will often" recognize them by shape (GR-3.3; the sentence is cut off in my extract).
- Choosing the anchor: the "principal ring system" is placed first, and then "all other substituents [are] positioned accordingly" (GR-3.4). It is chosen by most fused rings, then largest rings, then unsaturation, then heteroatoms (GR-3.4.1).
- Standard orientations by class: amino acids, carbohydrates, steroids, tetrapyrroles, carotenoids, and polymers each have class-specific drawing styles, and each is defined in its own IUPAC document (GR-3.6).

**Comparability across a set is explicit.** "In a discussion of several related molecules ... it would be reasonable to select a single ring system to be considered as 'most important' across the collection", for example benzoic acid across a series of 2-substituted benzoic acids, even where naphthyl would win in isolation (GR-3.4.1). "A group of related structures should be consistent in the style that is used among them" (GR-13.4). When relationships are being shown, as in complexes or reaction mechanisms, "it is entirely appropriate to modify the orientation of the individual diagrams so that they accurately describe whatever relationships are being depicted" (GR-3).

**One mark, one meaning.** "The end of one bond should not touch the end of another unless they truly are both bound to the same atom" (GR-0). Sharp corners inside a bond are not acceptable because "such angular bends within bonds normally imply CH2 groups" (GR-1.5). Dashed bonds for coordination are not acceptable because "such bonds have been used to indicate stereo-[chemistry]" (GR-1.7).

**Where position does carry meaning.** These are the conventions in which orientation is semantic:
- Fischer projection, IUPAC 2-Carb-3 (<https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/2carb/03n04.html>): "the carbon chain is written vertically, with the lowest numbered carbon atom at the top". For each carbon, "neighbouring carbon atoms are below, and the H and OH groups above the plane of the paper." Because the axes encode depth, an in-plane rotation changes meaning. That is why GR-3 names it as the exception.
- Haworth representation, 2-Carb-5.4 (<https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/2carb/05.html>): the ring is "orientated almost perpendicular to the plane of the paper", "the edge closer to the viewer is drawn below the more distant edge", "with the oxygen behind and C-1 at the right-hand end", giving "a clockwise numbering of the ring atoms."
- Fused ring orientation for numbering, FR-5.2 (<https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/fusedring/FR51.html>). The rules are applied in order: (a) "Maximum number of rings in a horizontal row"; (b) maximum rings in the upper right quadrant; (c) minimum in the lower left; (d) maximum above the row; (e) preferred peripheral numbering. This is a deterministic tie-break order that yields one orientation. The 2008 drawing guide says that orientation serves numbering and that drawing may depart from it "for aesthetic reasons" (GR-3.3.3).

**What a reader learns from shape alone:** ring systems and their fusion pattern, chain length and branching, and the location of functional groups (rightward by convention). For biomolecules in class orientations, stereochemistry is read from left and right positions.

**Algorithms.** IUPAC cites the depiction-algorithm literature without endorsing any one method: Weininger 1990, Helson 1999, Fricker et al. 2004, and Clark et al. 2006 (GR-0, refs 2–5). RDKit, a widely used open toolkit, has `Compute2DCoords(..., canonOrient=True)`, documented as "orient the molecule in a canonical way". Its `NormalizeDepiction` applies "a canonical transformation" that aligns the main axis with X ([RDKit rdDepictor docs](https://www.rdkit.org/docs/source/rdkit.Chem.rdDepictor.html)). So the industry normalizes orientation in code, while the standard stays advisory.

### 2. Electrical schematics

**Horowitz and Hill, "How to Draw Schematic Diagrams".** This is Appendix E of *The Art of Electronics*, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press 1980/1989. I read it as reprinted with permission at <https://xcircuit.sourceforge.net/goodschem/goodschem.html>.

- Fixed axes, held loosely. Hint 2: "In general, signals go from left to right; don't be dogmatic about this, though, if clarity is sacrificed." Hint 3: "Put positive supply voltages at the top of the page, negative at the bottom. Thus, npn transistors will usually have their emitter at the bottom, whereas pnp's will have their emitter topmost." So the vertical axis loosely means voltage and the horizontal axis loosely means signal causality.
- Grid. Rule 4: "Wires and components are aligned horizontally or vertically, unless there's a good reason to do otherwise."
- One mark, one meaning. Rule 1: "Wires connecting are indicated by a heavy black dot; wires crossing, but not connecting, have no dot". Rule 2: "Four wires must not connect at a point; i.e., wires must not cross and connect." Rule 3: "Always use the same symbol for the same device".
- Idioms for subunits. General Principle 2: "A good schematic makes circuit functions clear ... keep functional areas distinct ... There are conventional ways to draw functional subunits; don't draw a differential amplifier as in Figure E1, because the function won't be easily recognized. Likewise, flip-flops are usually drawn with clock and inputs on the left, set and clear on top and bottom, and outputs on the right." Hint 4 warns against routing every wire to the supply rails or a common ground wire.

**What a reader learns from shape:** the signal path from input on the left to output on the right, which stage feeds which, and supply polarity from vertical position. Familiar subcircuits (differential pair, flip-flop) are recognized as gestalts because each has a fixed drawing.

**IEC 61082-1:2014**, *Preparation of documents used in electrotechnology – Part 1: Rules*. The free IEC preview (<https://webstore.iec.ch/preview/info_iec61082-1%7Bed3.0%7Db.pdf>) gives only the table of contents. It confirms normative clauses on "7.1.1 Flow of energy, signal, etc.", "5.12.3 Orientation of symbols", and "7.4.2 Layout" of circuit diagrams, and a "Figure 21 – Example of functional grouping and signal flow directions". **I could not read the clause text**, so I make no claim about its exact wording. **IEEE Std 315-1975** (ANSI Y32.2) is the US symbol standard, and its scope is symbols rather than layout. I could not download a copy to check what it says about symbol orientation.

### 3. Hasse diagrams (order theory)

**Definition: the vertical axis is mandatory.** J. B. Nation, *Notes on Lattice Theory*, ch. 1 (<https://math.hawaii.edu/~jb/lat1-6.pdf>): "x is covered by y ... if x < y and there is no z ∈ P with x < z < y. It is clear that the covering relation determines the partial order in a finite ordered set ... the elements of P are represented by points in the plane, and a line is drawn from a up to b precisely when a ≺ b." His Figure 1.1 shows two different-looking diagrams, marked (d) ≅ (e), of isomorphic ordered sets. "The Hasse diagram of P^d is obtained by simply turning the Hasse diagram of P upside down". So one symmetry of the drawing, the vertical flip, is a meaningful operation: duality. R. Freese, "Automated Lattice Drawing" (<https://math.hawaii.edu/~ralph/Preprints/latdrawing.pdf>), defines it more strictly: "the edges are straight line segments and, if a < b in P, then the vertical coordinate for a is less than the one for b. Because of this second condition arrows are omitted." The axis replaces arrowheads. A drawing that breaks it is not a Hasse diagram.

**The horizontal axis is free, and freedom is the problem.** Freese again:
- "Generally lattice theorists have a fairly precise idea of how their diagrams should look." A mirror image "would be considered equally acceptable", but "while any permutation of the elements of a particular level would produce a correct representation of the order, almost any such permutation would make the diagram unrecognizable."
- "Most lattice theorists would draw the lattice of subsets of a three element set as the cube ... Orthomodular lattice theorists often prefer to draw it as in the center but no lattice theorist would draw it as is done on the right." Subcommunities have their own idioms.
- "Thus while a randomly chosen diagram will be mathematically correct, it is very likely to be useless. Prior to lattices being generated by computer programs this was not a problem: mathematicians knew ... how a particular lattice should be drawn and drew it that way."
- His first program produced diagrams in which "even small, well known lattices were usually unrecognizable."
- His algorithm: height comes from a deterministic rank function, `rank(a) = height(a) − depth(a) + M`, which gives "a nice top to bottom symmetry". Points of equal rank start on a circle in 3-space. Comparable elements attract and incomparable ones repel, in three phases, and then a 2D projection is chosen. The result: "While the results ... do not match the quality of hand drawn diagrams they are at least recognizably the same," and they come "very close to the hand drawn version" for 10 or fewer elements.
- On niceness: "What constitutes a 'nice' diagram is, of course, a question of æthetics, not mathematics." Wille's group proposed minimizing the number of distinct edge slopes, which Freese calls "very important", but "some minimum slope diagrams can be bizarre". Ganter and Wille's "rule of parallelograms and the rule of lines" refine this. Minimizing crossings is NP-hard. Freese proves it NP-complete for lattices even with rank heights and a fixed order of atoms (Theorem 1).

**What a reader learns from shape:** height is chain length, width is antichains, and turning the drawing upside down gives the dual. Named sub-shapes carry theorems. Dedekind: "A lattice is modular if and only if it does not contain the pentagon as a sublattice." Birkhoff: "A modular lattice is distributive if and only if it does not contain the diamond as a sublattice" (Nation, *Notes on Lattice Theory* ch. 9, Theorems 9.1–9.2, <https://math.hawaii.edu/~jb/lat7-12.pdf>). These sub-shapes have names because they are always drawn the same way.

Davey and Priestley's *Introduction to Lattices and Order* (2nd ed., CUP 2002) has a section titled "Diagrams: the art of drawing ordered sets" (p. 10, from the contents scan at <https://web.flu.cas.cz/scan/323537159.pdf>). I could not read its text.

### 4. Feynman diagrams

**From spacetime picture to topology.** D. Kaiser, "Physics and Feynman's Diagrams", *American Scientist* 93 (2005) 156–165, doi:10.1511/2005.52.156 (read at <https://chaosbook.org/library/FeynmDiagAmSci0305.pdf>). Kaiser is a historian. He summarizes his book *Drawing Theories Apart* (Chicago 2005).

- Feynman's first published diagram, from "Space-Time Approach to Quantum Electrodynamics" (*Phys. Rev.* 76, 1949), "represented events in two dimensions: space on the horizontal axis and time on the vertical axis." Figure caption: "Today most physicists draw Feynman diagrams in a more stylized way, highlighting the topology of propagation lines and vertices."
- The rule set: "At every 'vertex,' draw two electron lines meeting one photon line. Draw all of the topologically distinct ways that electrons and photons can scatter."
- Without shared rules, practice was unreadable. Bethe and Welton "failed to understand what Feynman was doing". In 1950 a student and two postdocs kept "getting different answers when using the diagrams for what was supposed to be the same calculation." Dyson's papers supplied "step-by-step instructions for how the diagrams should be drawn and how they were to be translated", and his papers were cited more often than Feynman's for years.
- Drift once anchoring loosened: physicists "tinkered with the diagrams—adding a new type of line here, dropping an earlier arrow convention there, adopting different labeling schemes ... Local schools emerged ... The diagrams drawn by graduate students at Cornell began to look more and more like each other and less like the diagrams drawn by students at Columbia or Rochester or Chicago."

**Line styles are fixed within a text, and identity is labeled topology.** D. Tong, *Quantum Field Theory* lecture notes, ch. 3 (Cambridge; <https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/qft/three.pdf>): "We'll choose dotted lines for mesons, and solid lines for nucleons ... add an arrow to solid lines to denote its charge". "Each such diagram you can draw is in 1-1 correspondence with the terms in the expansion". Two diagrams with the same unlabeled shape but swapped outgoing labels are distinct terms. Nucleon scattering has "'t-channel' and 'u-channel' diagrams", and "the first diagram indeed includes a vertex that looks like the letter 'T'". The channel names come from how the drawing looks under a convention about which side is incoming.

**Automatic layout.** J. Ellis, *TikZ-Feynman* v1.0.0 (2016), arXiv:[1601.05437](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.05437). It "delegates the positioning of vertices to algorithms" and lets the author orient the result (`horizontal=a to b`). "The spring layout algorithm attempts to 'spread out' the diagram as much as possible which—for most simpler diagrams—gives a satisfactory result". But "a good example where the spring layout doesn't work are decays where we have the decaying particle on the left and all the daughter particles on the right." The fallbacks come in order: invisible edges, a layered layout (which "does pay attention to the order in which vertices are declared"), and finally, "In more complicated diagrams, it is quite likely that none of the algorithms work ... In such cases, the vertices have to be placed manually."

**What a reader learns from shape:** vertex count (the perturbative order), loops, which particle is exchanged (from line style), and the channel (s, t, or u) from how the internal line sits relative to the incoming and outgoing sides.

### Cross-cutting table

| Notation | Identity lives in | Position fixed by | Free | Strength of convention |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical structure | bond graph and stereo marks | orientation preferences; ring templates; principal-ring anchor; class orientations | most placement; "art form" | advisory, except Fischer and Haworth (semantic) |
| Schematic | netlist | signal left to right; +V at top; grid; subcircuit idioms | placement within those | advisory ("don't be dogmatic"); IEC 61082-1 text unverified |
| Hasse | cover relation | up means greater (definitional); height often by rank | horizontal order | vertical axis mandatory; horizontal left to idiom |
| Feynman | labeled topology | originally time vertical; now incoming and outgoing sides by local convention | everything else | loose, and it drifted by school |
