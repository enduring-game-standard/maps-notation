# Raph Koster's "A Grammar of Gameplay" (GDC 2005)

Research for [issue #18](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/18). Feeds [issue #14](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/issues/14). Bura's side is covered in [bura-game-diagrams.md](https://github.com/enduring-game-standard/maps-notation/blob/research/bura-game-diagrams/research/bura-game-diagrams.md). This note covers Koster and the exchange between the two.

## What bears on the coherent design

- **Koster's atom is a nested challenge, not a Petri element.** An atom is a goal with a verb at its center. It has a topology, a success link, and a required failure link. It nests inside a parent challenge and contains smaller challenges. Success "pops" back to the parent, like a call stack. Recursion stops at verbs that need no skill and carry no risk: a UI action, "a verb outside the magic circle". A MAPS verb is Koster's *verb*. A Koster *atom* is closer to a MAPS sub-net around one verb with a success outcome and a failure outcome. So the atom is a good candidate for the unit MAPS folds: one box with an enter, a succeed exit, and a fail exit.
- **Composition has three kinds, and each has a measure.** Chaining (in sequence), recursion (nested), and parallelism (alternative challenges at once). Koster then defines *size* as the number of atoms chained in sequence, *depth* as how deep the recursion goes, and *breadth* as how much parallelism there is. Those are cheap at-a-glance invariants a folded MAPS design could report.
- **Nesting against sharing is the folding problem Koster left open, and he said so.** In 2007, Dan Cook argued that a directed graph beats strict nesting, because one atom ("jump") feeds several parents, and nesting duplicates it. Koster agreed it is "a question of info design". In 2018 he said flowchart notations, his own and Machinations included, "unroll" games and lose the nesting. His proposed fix is software where "you can click into a node and expand it". For MAPS: fold into referenced sub-nets (a DAG), not copied ones (a tree). Link's jump, roll, and shield are used in many contexts.
- **Comparison is by graph, scalars, and skin.** Koster's 2015 "family trees": a *clone* is the same graph with a new skin, *content* is the same graph with new scalars, a *variant* is a similar graph where some atoms change, a *family* is a collection of variants, and a *genre* is a collection of clones and variants. In 2018 he refines this: count how many atoms changed and *where in the nesting* they sit. This is a ready vocabulary for child Link against adult Link: mostly the same graph with different gear (scalars or filtered verbs), plus a few atom swaps.
- **The diagnostics are structural, and most are checkable on a net.** These are Koster's rules. Every risky atom must have a failure link. Only leaf atoms may have fixed outcomes. A loop back to the same atom is a valid loop (combos). A loop that pops the stack is suspect (an unbeatable combo). A loop that trends toward certainty is bad. An atom with too many branches is too hard to learn, and he suggests 7±2 abilities per atom as a rule of thumb. A long chain of UI actions with no branching "sticks out as unfun". Several of these line up with MAPS checks MAPS already has or could add (dead verbs, stalls, outcomes that cannot be reached).
- **Look: color carries meaning, and tangles are information.** In 2005, red marks a risk-free or fixed-outcome link or atom, and blue marks a risky one. In 2015, color gives the node kind: green for verbs, blue for statistical resources, red for in-world objects, and a dashed outline for friends' verbs. He also reads the layout as evidence: in the Frontierville graph "you can see what parts … were probably hard to balance -- right where it gets tangled". Koster gives no layout rules.
- **Time and topology are annotations on an atom, not layers.** Each atom carries a topology glyph (8x8, 4x4, a 224-unit axis, a 1-6 die) and a time class: "T" for turn-based or user-determined, "P" for set by the opponent, or a span in milliseconds. "All games are turn-based. The question is whether the opponent is playing the same atom you are." That bears on MAPS's open "Time" question: one turn model, with the turn length as a per-atom property.
- **What Koster did not solve:** an orthography ("We have grammar, but we don't have orthography"). Also execution semantics. Also how to notate families of tokens ("do I have to actually notate every single one of the 52 cards in poker?"). Also whether chess pieces are verbs or assets. And nesting against sharing. Bura answered by moving to flat resource flow, which made nesting implicit (colored Preparation tokens) rather than solving it.

**Net for MAPS:** Koster supplies the *hierarchy* that MAPS's flat net lacks: nested challenge atoms with enter, succeed, and fail interfaces, plus depth, breadth, and size as measures. He also supplies a comparison vocabulary (same graph, new scalars, new skin). Bura supplies the executable-looking flow. Neither joins the two. MAPS can: keep the anchored net as the leaf level, and let a Koster atom be a named, referenced fold over a sub-net whose exits are its success and failure outcomes.

## The talk's frame

- **Notation, not analysis.** Koster opens with dance (Laban notation), prosody, staff notation, harmonic analysis, and tablature (slides 3-8), then asks "Can we notate games? Yes, but I didn't succeed." (slide 9). Notation will "tackle only mechanics, with perhaps some hinting for dynamics" (slide 13). It works with *constituative* rules, while "Operational rules are for the audience, not fellow craftspeople" (slide 15). "All successful notation systems have been Visual, Iconic, Not textual. They are always new written languages" (slide 17). The talk owes the concept of "ludemes" to Ben Cousins (slide 18).
- **What it is not.** Not game maps, because topology is not the visual playfield. Not math, because "Mathematical balancing is a data issue, not a systemic issue". Not flowcharts, because "We're not tracing the game path from an experiential point of view". Not game-theory diagrams: "We're not interested in graphing the possibility space. Rather, we want to know the algorithm for the possibility space" (slides 19-20). His method is "reverse engineering" (slide 21), tested on phase-based RPG combat (slides 22-23).
- **The ingredients** (slide 24): Territory, Preparation, Core mechanic, Range of challenges, Choice of abilities, Skill required, Variable feedback, Dealing with the Mastery Problem, Cost of failure. "The whole game AND each NPC have these!"

## The atom and its ingredients

| Ingredient | Koster's definition (slide) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Territory (topology) | "Exposed information coupled to your game token." The operational space of an asset: all the vectors of force it can apply and that can apply to it (26). | Preparation | "Prior choices made that influence the next atom." (39) | Built on Crawford's conversation: "a cyclic process where two actors alternatively listen, think, and speak"; "All games must be perceived as iterative, looping" (38). | Core mechanic (ludeme) | "Verbs are the nucleus. Without a verb, there is no atom." (56) | Rejects the Fullerton/Swain/Hoffman objectives list as not atomic and as ignoring topology (57-58). | Skill required | Nesting ends "when there is no skill involved in executing the verb", with certainty, risk, and uncertainty distinguished (79). | |
| Variable feedback | "There must be more than one possible outcome to the successful completion of the challenge in an atom, prior to the execution of the next atom." (82) | "Only 'leaf' atoms should have fixed outcomes!" (93). |

Worked diagrams:
- **Galaxian** (48). "Get highest score" (a 224-wide axis) forks into parallel "Kill enemy" (var secs.) → "Position token" → "Press fire button" (red leaf), and "Don't get hit" (Pvar <256) → "Move" (red leaf). "Parallel, recursive, and sequential!" The vertical screen axis "was time".
- **Chess against checkers** (67). Chess: "Capture king" (8x8) → "Move piece" (var) → "Physical movement". Checkers: "Capture all pieces" (4x4) forks to "Capture one piece" (2x2) and "Create forced jump" (var), each → "Physical movement". "So why is chess harder and deeper?" The point is that the two skeletons are nearly the same, and the difference is in the abilities (71: every checker piece has the same topology, chess pieces "radically different topologies").
- **Spotting bad atoms** (80). Crafting → Harvesting → Killing / Finding; Crafting → Dragging → Combining (red); Selling → Pricing. "Never link or nest too many base atoms".
- **Checkers, "my best take so far"** (108). It adds ability counters (A=12 per side), Force jump and Capture one as 2x2 sub-atoms, the opponent's counter decrementing, and promotion "Ac = king" feeding back to Capture all. This is the diagram Bura says he reacted to.

## How atoms compose

- **Three relations** (42-47). Chaining: "Games can be seen as sequenced events" (Moon Patrol, slide 45). Recursion: "nested events. A given challenge is part of a meta-challenge, or includes challenges within itself" (Moon Patrol: highest score, within which reach the other side, within which defeat an enemy, within which time a button press, slide 46). Parallelism: "multiple challenges undertaken at once" (checkers: remove all, or remove one, or set up a defense, or set up an attack, slide 47).
- **Bounds.** At the top: "All games have at least one level of recursion. The last level is the 'magic circle'". Games without a single top goal are "play", as with MMORPGs and "software toys" (49). At the bottom: "Fundamental atoms will be ones demanding no skill, providing no risk of failure … a verb outside the magic circle" (64).
- **Measures** (52). "Depth: literally, the depth of recursion. Breadth: literally, the amount of parallelism. Size: literally, the amount of sequentially chained atoms." Breadth drives how fast a player groks a game (50). "All games are perceived sequentially. Most games, however, are not mentally played sequentially!" (51).
- **Chunking** (66). "If one atom has too many branches, then we have a problem. Chunking is implicit in atom construction: 'Move' may break down into many possible directions, but those can be chunked." This is the nearest Koster comes to folding a family. He folds it by declaring it one atom, and gives no mechanism.
- **Opponents** (85-87). "The most basic sort of variable feedback is your opponent's move." "All games are turn-based. The question is whether the opponent is playing the same atom you are." Checkers is symmetrical: "they have the same diagram". Galaxian and Fox & Geese are not. Loops can be "Imperative (skips opponent)", such as uninterruptible combos and triple jumps, or "Sequential (returns to turn-based)".

## What Koster said was unsolved

- 2005: "So I didn't finish… We have grammar, but we don't have orthography." (99). "This is all mostly useless right now. I can't yet picture designing a game from this notation … Notation is a capture tool primarily." (101). What it gives: "a heuristic … the concept of pitch", "an atomic model [that] allows us to examine logical links and feedback loops clearly", and "a vocabulary … depth, breadth, size, cheat, content, playfield" (102). Open challenges: finding degenerate strategies the way music finds parallel fifths, whether notation can show emergence, and practicality: "What's faster, notating a rhythm game or writing 'rhythm game'?" (100).
- 2005 reception: "significantly more mixed reactions to A Grammar of Gameplay than there were to the keynote" (GDC round-up). In 2007 he called it "my lowest-rated lecture ever … because it was split" (Gamasutra interview).
- 2007, on token families and asset or verb: "Are the pieces in chess … verbs, or are they assets you manipulate? … 'Well, do I have to actually notate every single one of the 52 cards in poker, or what?'" Also: "It's easy to look at something like Space Invaders and do that breakdown... It's really hard to do it for poker". And on what notation shows: "The pieces did not give me a recipe for fun. They gave me a really good way to see when something wouldn't be fun … They show you the absence of stuff more than the presence." (Gamasutra interview).
- 2007, nesting against graph (Cooking up Chemistry and its comments). Koster: Cook's skill chains "seem to be largely linear … I … tend to see these as nested. In my model, the atoms are nested pretty much indefinitely and arbitrarily." Cook: "A directed graph ends up being a bit more flexible when a single atom feeds into multiple other higher level atoms … Strict nesting … can get quite messy with many duplicated atoms if you show the contents all the time. The opposite problem is that if you don't show the contents, it becomes harder to visualize the relationships". Koster: "it's definitely a question of info design … I tend to think of it organizationally … start graphing the game at the highest level, largest challenges, and decompose down until you reach the trivial challenges."
- 2006-2008: the book *A Grammar of Gameplay: How Games Work* was announced (2006-05-19) and was "not been published – or finished being written!" as of 2008-11-11. I found no later publication.
- 2018 (AAAI-18 transcript): "A lot of the work around loops and grammar, including my stabs at notation systems and things like Machinations, tend to unroll games into flowcharts. Those tend to not capture this nesting quality. It can be represented, of course, particularly using software as an interface. You can click into a node and expand it and see that there's a self-similar machine deeper down." He also says the project "ended up being something relatively informal. There wasn't anything as rigorous as a BNF grammar".

## Koster's later notation (2015)

The PAXDev 2015 deck is his mature form, and it differs from 2005:
- **Three node kinds**: green circle = verb, blue box = statistical resource, red rounded box = in-world object, dashed green = a friend's verb (slide 54 of that deck). Arrows run between them. Most arrows alternate between a verb and a noun, but I did not check every arc. Each graph comes with counts ("Verbs: 3 (+3), Objects: 3, Stats: 3" for Farmville) and a bar chart comparing five social games by those counts (slides 55-60).
- **Comparison by graph.** "Island Life had a pretty subtle difference from Farmville, which was adding the ability to sculpt your island by buying land", and the diagram shows it as one added object (Land), with the verb count going from 3 to 4. "It's funny how the graph isn't actually very different at all between Atari Tennis and Virtua Tennis." Family trees: "Clone: same graph, new skin. Content: same graph, new scalars. Variant: similar graph, some atoms change. Family: a collection of variants. Genre: a collection of clones and variants" (slide 75).
- **Claimed payoffs**: "you can instantly see if your game is too hard, too simple, too repetitive, has a boring patch, has parts that can be cut. It also outputs your object classes (!) It is self-documenting, hang it on the wall." (slide 70).
- **Positioning**: "If it's math, it can be graphed. The most extensively developed method is Machinations, … for problems expressible as economies and calculus. On the other hand, topology … is better expressed using graph theory." (slides 52-53). Bura, Cook, Dormans and Adams, and Burgun are listed as "fellow travelers" (slide 2). The nesting idea survives as "games are made of games", and "building blocks that form an entire learning loop (aka atom) in their own right are termed a 'mechanic'" (captions page).

## The Koster-Bura exchange

- **Direction.** Bura's article opens: "This is a reaction to Raph Koster's GDC 2005 presentation". He adopts Koster's constituative-rules stance ("as Raph posits"), the napkin goal ("Doodle a game on a napkin, as Raph puts it"), the ingredient list, and checkers as the test case "since it's the example used in Raph's talk".
- **What Bura changed.** Koster's diagram is a *hierarchy of challenges*: boxes nest, success pops, and failure loops back. Bura's is a *flat resource flow* (Petri-like boxes, transitions, sources, sinks, inhibitors). He keeps Preparation as a token resource, and he turns Koster's composition relations into token colors: "Using colors for the preparation tokens and the transitions that generate and consume them let us describe high-level processes: Chaining (plot sequence, combos), recursion (multi-level resources combination in crafting), or parallelism (attack & defense, skill trees and stats). Yet, for the purpose of producing napkin diagrams, treating preparation as one resource is often enough." So nesting becomes implicit in which colored tokens a transition needs. Bura never draws it.
- **Koster's response** was brief: "Stéphane Bura has picked up the ball and run with it! Check out his Game Diagrams based on the grammar I proposed." (2006-03-30). In that thread a commenter, Patrick Dugan, names "encapsulation" as "the real obstacle … what of the electrons that enable these ludemes to interface?" Bura answers that resource boxes are "the potential covalent bonds … already shared between several sub-graphs". Koster did not comment in the thread. Later he apparently showed a Bura diagram in his 2007 Austin GDC talk and called it more complex than his or Cook's ("it's not near as complex as the Stéphane Bura one that I showed", Gamasutra 2007). He linked Bura in 2008 and listed him as a fellow traveler in 2015 and 2018.
- **What neither solved.** (1) An interface for composing pieces. Koster has nesting with no shared sub-atoms. Bura has shared boxes with no nesting. (2) Families of tokens: Koster's "52 cards" and Bura's colored-token box, which is stated once and never drawn. (3) Semantics: Koster has none, and Bura's are informal. Neither can be executed. (4) Layout: neither gives rules beyond color and flow direction.
